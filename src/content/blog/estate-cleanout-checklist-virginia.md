If you are reading this, someone has probably died, or moved into care, and the house is now your responsibility. This is the practical sequence.

## Before anything moves

Four things come first, and they come before a single box is opened.

**Secure the property.** Change the locks if there is any doubt about who holds keys — this is not paranoia, it is a fiduciary obligation. Confirm the homeowner's insurance is still active and, critically, that the carrier knows the property is now vacant. Many policies restrict or void coverage on a home unoccupied beyond thirty or sixty days, and discovering that after a burst pipe is a genuinely expensive way to learn it.

**Do not throw anything out yet.** Not one bag. It is very common for a family member to start with what looks obviously worthless and later discover that the box of papers included a savings bond, a life insurance policy or a deed.

**Find the will and the financial paperwork.** Usually a desk, a filing cabinet, a bedroom safe, or a bank safe deposit box. In Virginia the will is filed with the Circuit Court in the jurisdiction where the person lived — Fairfax, Loudoun, Prince William and Arlington each have their own probate process and their own clerk's office.

**Establish your authority.** Until qualified as executor or administrator, you generally should not be disposing of estate property at all. Get that in place first; it protects you.

## The sweep, before the sort

Before any sorting, walk the house once with an empty box and a specific mindset: find the things that are easy to lose.

Cash inside books, envelopes and coat pockets. Jewellery in dresser drawers, jars and shoeboxes. Coins, which turn up in surprising volumes in this generation's houses. Military discharge papers — DD-214s especially, which are needed for burial benefits and are irreplaceable in practice. Savings bonds, stock certificates, deeds, titles and insurance policies. Photographs and letters. Anything handwritten.

Older houses in Vienna, Arlington and Leesburg produce a striking amount of this, because the people who lived in them did not bank digitally and did not throw much away.

## Then the professionals, in order

Sequence matters here, and getting it wrong costs money.

**Appraiser first**, if there is any possibility of items with real value — antiques, art, jewellery, firearms, collections. An appraisal before anything leaves is worth far more than a guess afterwards, and estate accounting frequently requires it.

**Estate sale company second.** They will tell you honestly whether there is enough to warrant a sale. If there is, they run it and take a percentage.

**Cleanout crew third**, for everything that did not sell. This is almost always considerably more than anyone expects — estate sales typically move a fraction of a house's contents.

Doing this in the wrong order means valuable items get hauled and worthless items get appraised.

## Firearms have their own rules

Worth stating plainly because it comes up more often than people expect, particularly in Prince William and Loudoun.

Firearms in an estate cannot simply be included in a cleanout. They need to be transferred through a licensed dealer, and the rules differ depending on who is inheriting and where they live. No hauling company can legally take them.

If you find one, secure it and call a licensed dealer or the estate attorney. Do not put it in a pile.

## Donation receipts and estate accounting

If the estate is claiming charitable deductions — and it often should be — the paperwork has to be right.

Ask for **itemised receipts**, not a generic acknowledgment slip. The receipt should list what was donated in enough detail to support a valuation. Tell your cleanout crew before they load, not afterwards, because sorting for donation and documenting it is a different job from simply hauling.

Keep the receipts with the estate records. Your accountant will want them and reconstructing this later is unpleasant.

## What a good crew does differently

The physical work is not the differentiator on estate jobs. The protocol is.

Anything that looks personal should be set aside rather than loaded — documents, photographs, jewellery, cash, military records, anything handwritten. A good crew over-flags dramatically. Nobody has ever been upset that a shoebox of old receipts was saved for review; the opposite mistake cannot be undone.

The pace should also be yours. Starting in a garage or basement rather than a bedroom is standard practice for a reason: it builds momentum without asking anyone to make emotionally difficult decisions in the first hour.

## Realistic timelines

A modest townhouse: one long day, one or two loads.

A four-bedroom single-family home with a full basement, attic and garage: two to three days, two to three loads. This is the most common Northern Virginia estate property and it is consistently underestimated.

Hoarding-condition properties: quoted separately, worked in stages, often across several visits and frequently alongside a therapist or social worker already involved with the family. Compressing these does not work and attempting it causes real harm.

## If you are doing this from out of state

Extremely common here, and it works fine.

The property gets walked on video, scope and price agreed remotely, and access arranged through a neighbour, a realtor or the estate attorney. Photographs come back at the end of each day. Keepsake items get boxed and either held for collection or shipped.

You do not need to fly in to point at furniture.

## Coordinating with a listing date

If the property is being sold, the cleanout sits on the critical path and the calendar is unforgiving. Northern Virginia listing windows move quickly and a clearing that slips a week can cost a seller real money.

Work backwards from the listing date: clearing, then any repairs, then cleaning, then staging, then photography. Tell your cleanout crew the listing date rather than just asking when they are available, and let them talk directly to the agent. Relaying logistics between three parties is how dates get missed.
